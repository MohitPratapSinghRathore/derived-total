\begin{abstract}
Language models degrade over long reasoning chains, and the accuracy drop is
usually reported as one number. That number confounds two failures with opposite
remedies: the model has lost track of the world, or it holds the world correctly
and chooses badly. We separate them in a setting with an exact external state
oracle, chess from move notation with no search at inference, by reading linear
probes against the true position at every token. Three findings follow. First,
aggregate state fidelity, the quantity this literature normally reports, barely
predicts behaviour: within a fixed reasoning depth, the correlation between the
number of misremembered squares and playing an illegal move ranges from
\result{corrGlobalMinAttn} to \result{corrGlobalMaxAttn}. Second, the same
quantity measured locally predicts it strongly: the probe is wrong on precisely
the squares a move touches for \result{locIllAttn} of illegal moves against
\result{locLegAttn} of legal ones. Third, and centrally, the errors are coherent
rather than random. Reconstructing the position the model appears to believe, we
find \result{belIllAttn} of its illegal moves are legal in that believed
position, against \result{belMisAttn} for the same move scored under another
position's belief at equal depth and \result{belRandAttn} for an arbitrary
illegal move. The model is not emitting noise. It is playing correctly on a board
it has misremembered. We give the measurement protocol, report a failed
prediction and a degenerate metric openly, and evaluate one architectural
intervention with it.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} long-horizon reasoning, state tracking, world models,
probing, model diagnostics, design science

\section{Introduction}

A model that reasons over many steps has to keep track of something. A
Transformer stores nothing between positions. Each token's representation is
rebuilt from the whole prefix through attention \citep{vaswani2017attention}, so
any quantity the task requires, a board position, the value of a variable, which
sub-goal remains open, exists only because the network re-derives it, at every
layer and every position, from the beginning. That re-derivation is dependable
over short spans and unreliable over long ones. Anyone who has watched a model
reason for forty steps knows the symptom: it stays fluent and quietly stops being
right about its own earlier commitments.

The usual remedies sit outside the model. Tree search grafts deliberation onto
the policy \citep{yao2023tree}. Process reward models supervise intermediate
steps with a learned verifier \citep{lightman2024lets}. Self-consistency samples
several chains and votes \citep{wang2023selfconsistency}. Each raises end-task
accuracy, and each leaves the original question untouched: what fails inside the
network as the chain lengthens? The question matters commercially as well as
scientifically, because the two candidate failures recommend opposite purchases.
If the model has forgotten the world, a verifier that rescores its outputs is
paying repeatedly to repair something the architecture gave away. If the model
remembers the world and reasons badly over it, a verifier is exactly right.

Answering the question requires reading an intermediate representation against a
known-correct intermediate state, and on open-ended text no such state exists.
The intermediate ``state'' of an argument in prose is not well defined, which is
why evaluation in this area is noisy and why claims about it are hard to falsify.
Our response is to move the measurement to a domain where the state is exact,
cheap, and external, establish what can be learned there, and be explicit about
what that does and does not license.

\paragraph{What we find.}
Working with search-free autoregressive models trained only on chess move
notation, we measure the internal state against a rules engine at every token and
compare it with what the model actually does. Three results structure the paper.

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Aggregate state fidelity is a poor predictor of behaviour.} Probe
accuracy over the whole board, which is the standard reported quantity, correlates
with illegal-move production at only \result{corrGlobalMinAttn} to
\result{corrGlobalMaxAttn} within a fixed depth. A model can have lost a great
deal of the board and still play legally, and can have most of it and still fail.
\item \textbf{Action-relevant state fidelity is a strong predictor.} Restricting
attention to the squares a move actually touches, the probe is wrong for
\result{locIllAttn} of illegal moves and \result{locLegAttn} of legal ones. What
matters is not how much of the world has been lost but whether the part about to
be used has been lost.
\item \textbf{The errors are coherent.} We reconstruct the board the model appears
to believe and ask whether its illegal move is legal there. It is, for
\result{belIllAttn} of illegal moves, with a bootstrap interval of
[\result{belIllLoAttn}, \result{belIllHiAttn}]. The same move scored against
another position's believed board at equal depth is legal
\result{belMisAttn} of the time, and an arbitrary illegal move scored against the
model's own believed board is legal \result{belRandAttn} of the time. Lossy
decoding caps what is detectable at \result{belLegAttn}, the rate for moves that
were genuinely legal.
\end{enumerate}

The third result is the one we would defend hardest. Long-horizon failure in
these models is not noise breaking through as the context grows. It is competent
play in a world that has drifted, which is a claim about memory rather than about
reasoning, and it explains why fluency survives correctness: the model is doing
the right thing, in the wrong place.

\paragraph{Why chess.}
Chess presented purely as move notation, with no tree search at inference, gives
what prose does not. The true state after any prefix is computable by a rules
engine, so probing has a per-token label. Legality is decidable, so a
state-tracking failure has an unambiguous observable rather than a judgement
call. Move quality is separately scoreable by an engine, so the second failure
mode can be measured conditional on the first being absent. Depth is uniformly
scaled, unlike ``number of reasoning steps'' in prose. And the domain is
combinatorial, so surface heuristics do not substitute for computation. Chess is
our instrument, not our subject, and we are explicit in
Section~\ref{sec:limits} about the reach that buys.

\paragraph{Research questions and objectives.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{RQ1.} Can long-horizon accuracy loss be separated into state
tracking and planning, and estimated separately?
\textbf{RO1:} define both and give an estimator for each
(Sections~\ref{sec:formal}, \ref{sec:diag}).
\item \textbf{RQ2.} Does internal state loss explain behavioural failure, or
merely accompany it?
\textbf{RO2:} test the link per token, at the level of the specific state a given
action depends on (Sections~\ref{sec:coupling}, \ref{sec:belief}).
\item \textbf{RQ3.} How far does a search-free model reason before its state stops
being recoverable, and how does that depth respond to scale?
\textbf{RO3:} measure the horizon across a model ladder against trivial baselines
(Sections~\ref{sec:decay}, \ref{sec:scale}).
\item \textbf{RQ4.} Does the diagnostic discriminate between architectural
interventions?
\textbf{RO4:} evaluate a fixed-width recurrent state channel with
parameter-matched and ablated controls (Section~\ref{sec:alsb}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item A per-token decomposition of long-horizon error into state-tracking and
planning components, with an exact oracle rather than a learned verifier.
\item The finding that aggregate probe accuracy, the standard reported measure of
a world model, is close to uninformative about behaviour, while the same measure
restricted to action-relevant state is not. This changes what practitioners
should measure.
\item The belief-consistency result: long-horizon errors are coherent actions on
a decayed state, established against mismatched-belief and random-move controls,
and supported causally by an intervention that induces a false belief and changes
the action accordingly, with a calibrated edit strength and a norm-matched
control.
\item An honest account of two methodological failures, a degenerate metric and
an unlearnable task design, both of which shaped the protocol and neither of
which is usually reported.
\item Transferable design knowledge for building such diagnostics wherever an
external state oracle can be constructed.
\end{enumerate}

We present this as design-science research in the sense of
\citet{hevner2004design} and \citet{peffers2007design}, where the contribution is
an artifact together with the design knowledge it carries
\citep{gregor2013positioning}.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Inference-time search.}
Deliberation over candidates \citep{yao2023tree}, process supervision
\citep{lightman2024lets}, and sampling with a vote
\citep{wang2023selfconsistency}, built on chain-of-thought prompting
\citep{wei2022chain}, all buy accuracy with inference compute and take the base
policy as given. Our concern is the base policy. A verifier that spends its
budget repairing state-tracking errors is compensating for a representational
defect at a recurring cost. What we do differently is diagnose which defect is
present before recommending a remedy.

\paragraph{Long-context evaluation.}
Position-dependent retrieval failure \citep{liu2024lost} and effective-context
measurement \citep{hsieh2024ruler} show usable context is shorter than advertised
context. Both measure retrieval of material present in the prefix. Long-horizon
reasoning failure is different: the needed quantity was never written down, only
implied, and has to be maintained through composition. A model can attend to
every token of a sixty-ply game and still not know where the pieces are. What we
do differently is measure maintenance rather than retrieval.

\paragraph{World models and probing.}
Linear probes recovering board state from game transcripts
\citep{li2023emergent}, the linear structure of those representations
\citep{nanda2023emergent}, chess as a state-tracking testbed
\citep{toshniwal2022chess}, and causal editing of internal associations
\citep{meng2022locating} together establish that sequence models form internal
world models and that intervening on them changes behaviour. That literature
answers whether a world model exists. We take its existence as given and ask a
different question: how the fidelity of that world model varies with reasoning
depth, and whether its failures explain the model's behavioural failures at the
level of individual actions. Probe design follows the control-task discipline of
\citet{hewitt2019designing}. To our knowledge the belief-consistency test of
Section~\ref{sec:belief}, which asks whether a wrong action is right in the
model's own decoded world, has not been reported.

\paragraph{Expressivity of state.}
Theory bounds what these architectures can track: state-space models are weaker
than their recurrent form suggests \citep{merrill2024illusion}, and intermediate
tokens change what a Transformer can express \citep{merrill2024expressive}. That
work asks what is representable in principle. We ask what a trained model
actually maintains, and where it stops.

\paragraph{Recurrence and state-space models.}
Structured state-space models \citep{gu2022efficiently} and selective variants
\citep{gu2024mamba} give constant per-token state, usually motivated by
throughput and context length. We use a small supervised recurrent channel not as
a contribution in itself but as a test case for whether the diagnostic
discriminates between architectures.

\paragraph{Search-free chess models.}
Policies that reach strong play by distilling engine evaluations without a tree
\citep{ruoss2024amortized} show the task is learnable without search. Their
objective is strength; ours is measurement, so we deliberately study models weak
enough to fail. A model that never loses the position teaches nothing about
losing it.

\section{Design Problem and Requirements}
\label{sec:design}

Stated plainly: long-horizon failure is reported as a single number, so it cannot
be acted on. A practitioner watching accuracy fall at step thirty cannot tell
whether to buy a larger model, add a verifier, or change the architecture,
because the measurement does not distinguish the causes. The artifact is a
measurement procedure.

\paragraph{In scope.} Search-free autoregressive decoders; domains with an exact,
cheaply computable state oracle; state of bounded description length.

\paragraph{Out of scope.} Inference-time search, which we neither use nor
evaluate; tasks whose state grows without bound; and claims about
production-scale models, which our compute does not license.

\section{Problem Formalisation}
\label{sec:formal}

Let a task be a token sequence $x_{1:L}$ generated left to right, and
$s_t = \Phi(x_{1:t})$ the exact state after the prefix, from an external oracle
$\Phi$. For chess this is piece placement, side to move, castling rights, and the
en-passant target. The state space is large, but the description length of any
$s_t$ is constant in $t$: a position occupies a few hundred bits however many
plies produced it.

Let $h_t^{(\ell)}$ be the residual-stream activation at position $t$, layer
$\ell$, and $g^{(\ell)}$ a linear decoder trained to recover $s_t$ from it. Write
$\hat{s}_t^{(\ell)} = g^{(\ell)}(h_t^{(\ell)})$ for the decoded, or believed,
state.

\begin{definition}[State fidelity]
$F^{(\ell)}(t) = \Pr[\hat{s}_t^{(\ell)} = s_t]$, the probability the exact state
is linearly decodable at depth $t$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Action-relevant fidelity]
For an action $a$ touching a set of state components $R(a)$, the probability that
$\hat{s}_t$ and $s_t$ agree on $R(a)$. For a chess move this is the origin and
destination squares.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Error decomposition]
State-tracking error $E_{\mathrm{state}}(t)=\Pr[\hat{s}_t \neq s_t]$, observable
through the illegal-move rate. Planning error
$E_{\mathrm{plan}}(t)=\Pr[\text{blunder} \mid \hat{s}_t = s_t]$, from engine
centipawn loss conditional on intact state. Observed error is
$E_{\mathrm{state}} + (1-E_{\mathrm{state}})E_{\mathrm{plan}}$, and benchmarks
report only that sum.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Belief consistency]
An action is \emph{belief-consistent} when it is invalid in $s_t$ but valid in
$\hat{s}_t$. The rate of belief consistency among invalid actions distinguishes
coherent action on a wrong state from incoherent action.
\end{definition}

\section{The State-Space Bottleneck}
\label{sec:bottleneck}

\begin{proposition}[Cost mismatch]
For an attention-only decoder, computing $h_t^{(\ell)}$ attends over $t$ prior
positions, so maintaining state costs $O(t)$ reads and $O(t)$ key-value memory
while the state has description length $O(1)$. Nothing amortises it: $s_t$ and
$s_{t+1}$ differ by one move and are computed independently.
\end{proposition}

Two consequences were testable and we tested both. Fidelity should fall with
depth even well inside the context window, because the re-derivation circuit must
compose more updates within a fixed layer budget. It does
(Section~\ref{sec:decay}).

We also predicted fidelity would peak at intermediate layers and fall toward the
output, the state being a means rather than an end. \textbf{That prediction is
wrong.} Decodability rises through the stack and is maximal at the final block,
from \result{layerProfFirst} at the first layer to \result{layerProfLast} at the
last. For this task the state sits close to the target: the next legal move is a
direct function of the position, so a representation making the position linearly
available is also a good representation for the output head. We keep the failed
prediction because it constrains where a state carrier should be inserted, and
our spacing was chosen before we knew.

\section{Diagnostic Protocol}
\label{sec:diag}

\paragraph{Data.} Public game records above a rating floor, deduplicated by
move-sequence hash, split by \emph{game} so no prefix is shared between probe
fitting and evaluation. The corpus holds \result{nGames} games:
\result{ntrainlm} for training, \result{ntrainprobe} for probes, \result{neval}
for evaluation, mean length \result{meanPly} plies, move vocabulary
\result{nVocab}.

\paragraph{Oracle.} A rules engine replays every game and emits the exact state
after every token, verified complete before any model is measured.

\paragraph{Probes.} One linear head per layer, fitted on the probe split and
reported on the evaluation split. Because most squares are empty, raw per-square
accuracy is misleadingly high, so we report accuracy on occupied squares against
a per-square, per-depth majority predictor.

\paragraph{Controls.} A majority predictor; a label-permutation control pairing
activations with another game's state at equal depth, preserving marginals while
destroying the relation to the input; and a randomised-weight model of identical
architecture, bounding what probe capacity alone extracts.

\paragraph{Behavioural measures.} Illegal top-1 move rate against depth, and the
probability mass on legal continuations, both read against the oracle.

\subsection{Aggregate fidelity does not explain behaviour}
\label{sec:coupling}

The two curves fall together, and it would be easy to stop there and call them
one phenomenon. They are not, at least not in the form usually reported. Within a
fixed depth bucket, so that both quantities cannot simply be tracking depth, the
correlation between the number of misremembered squares and whether the model
plays an illegal move ranges from \result{corrGlobalMinAttn} to
\result{corrGlobalMaxAttn}. Aggregate board fidelity, the number this literature
reports as evidence of a world model, carries almost no information about whether
the next action will be valid.

We think this deserves emphasis because it is a negative result about a standard
practice. Reporting that a probe recovers a world model at some accuracy says
little about whether that model is load-bearing.

\subsection{Action-relevant fidelity does}

Restricting the measurement to the squares a move touches changes the picture.
The probe is wrong on those squares for \result{locIllAttn} of illegal moves and
\result{locLegAttn} of legal moves. The relevant question is not how much of the
world the model has lost but whether it has lost the part it is about to use.

\subsection{The errors are coherent}
\label{sec:belief}

If state loss drives the failure, an invalid action should be valid in the state
the model believes it is in. We reconstruct that believed position from the probe
and re-score the model's own move in it, clearing castling rights so nothing is
licensed by an assumption we did not decode, and testing pseudo-legality because
a decoded board may lack a king.

Of the illegal moves the model plays, \result{belIllAttn} are legal in its own
believed position, with a bootstrap interval of [\result{belIllLoAttn},
\result{belIllHiAttn}]. Two controls bound the alternatives. The same move scored
against a believed position decoded elsewhere at equal depth is legal
\result{belMisAttn} of the time, so the effect is specific to this belief at this
position rather than to plausible boards generally. An arbitrary illegal move
scored against the model's own believed position is legal \result{belRandAttn} of
the time, so the believed board is not permissive. The ceiling on what this test
can detect is \result{belLegAttn}, the rate at which genuinely legal moves are
recovered as legal in the believed board, which is below one because probe
decoding is lossy.

Against that ceiling, roughly half of the model's illegal moves are coherent
actions in a misremembered world, and the enrichment over the matched control is
a factor of \result{belRatioMisAttn}. This is what we mean by coherent error, and
it is the clearest statement we can make that long-horizon degradation here is a
memory failure rather than a reasoning failure.

\subsection{An intervention, evaluated by the diagnostic}
\label{sec:alsb}

The diagnostic is only useful if it discriminates. We test a fixed-width gated
recurrent channel inserted after every $k$-th attention block, carrying
$z_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_s}$ with $d_s \ll d$:
\[
u_t = W_{\mathrm{in}} h_t, \quad
\alpha_t = \sigma(W_g[h_t; z_{t-1}]), \quad
z_t = (1-\alpha_t)\odot A z_{t-1} + \alpha_t \odot \phi(u_t),

separate architecture ($\lambda=0$), supervision ($\lambda>0$), and read-out
(zeroing $W_{\mathrm{out}}$ at test time), against a parameter-matched
attention-only control whose feed-forward block absorbs the same parameter count.

\subsection{Inducing a false belief changes the action}
\label{sec:causal}

Belief consistency is observational. It shows the model errs coherently with a
decayed state without showing that the state representation drives the action.
We therefore intervene. At a position where the side to move genuinely occupies a
square, we push the residual stream along the probe direction encoding "this
square is empty" and ask whether the model stops proposing moves from it.

Two safeguards decide whether such an experiment means anything, and both are
reported. The manipulation check asks whether the edit changed the decoded belief
at all: at the strength we use it does, in \result{patchFlipAttn} of cases. The
norm-matched control applies a random direction of identical magnitude at the
same position, which separates belief from mere perturbation sensitivity.

Edit strength has to be calibrated rather than chosen, because a large enough
push degrades the forward pass and will move behaviour for reasons having nothing
to do with state. Table~\ref{tab:patchcal} reports the sweep. Below a quarter of
the residual-stream magnitude the edit does not reliably flip the belief; above
roughly one, the random control also starts destroying the output distribution,
so any effect there is damage rather than belief. We report the intervention at
\result{patchAlphaAttn}, inside the window where the manipulation succeeds and
the control does not.

At that strength, editing the state direction removes \result{patchCutAttn} of
the probability mass on moves from the affected square, decreasing it in
\result{patchDecAttn} of \result{patchNAttn} cases. The norm-matched random edit
removes \result{patchRandCutAttn} and decreases the mass in
\result{patchRandDecAttn} of cases, which is indistinguishable from a coin flip.
The state representation is not a decorative correlate of the model's
computation. It is read.

\subsection{A second domain that did not work, and why}
\label{sec:synthfail}

We attempted a second oracle-bearing domain, a program updating integer variables
with interleaved queries, to test whether these findings are properties of
long-horizon state tracking or of chess. Four designs failed to leave chance, and
we report the sequence because the diagnosis is more useful than the intention.

The first placed a single query at the end of a sequence of random updates.
Training loss settled at the token-type entropy of the generator, which is the
value a model achieves by learning the format and nothing else, and the reason is
arithmetic: the only learnable signal was one answer token in roughly one
hundred and thirty, so state tracking carried under one percent of the gradient.
Interleaving queries raised that share and did not move accuracy. Reducing the
modulus and the number of variables did not move it either. A final version
separated a pure read-out query from a computed one, so that state tracking could
succeed without the arithmetic succeeding, and read-out accuracy still sat at
chance.

We draw two conclusions rather than one. The narrow conclusion is that this task
family asks a small model to learn modular arithmetic before any state tracking
becomes observable, and that our compute did not reach it. The broader one is a
warning for anyone building synthetic state-tracking benchmarks: a task can be
perfectly well specified, carry an exact oracle, and still measure nothing,
because the quantity of interest contributes almost none of the training signal.
The loss curve diagnoses this cheaply. A model that has learned the format and no
content sits at the generator's token-type entropy, which is computable in
advance and worth computing before trusting such a benchmark. We report this in
place of a cross-domain result, which we do not have.

\subsection{A degenerate metric, declared}
Our horizon was pre-registered on exact-position fidelity. That quantity reaches
zero by roughly ply \result{exactPly} in every condition, so it cannot
discriminate between them, and conditioning on it is impossible because exact
states essentially do not occur past the opening. We therefore report horizons on
occupied-square accuracy and on the illegal-move rate. The revision was made
after inspecting baseline data and before evaluating any intervention, and every
definition is applied unchanged across conditions. We flag it rather than
presenting the revised metric as the original plan, and we note that anyone
measuring state tracking with an all-or-nothing criterion will obtain an
unusable number for the same reason.

\section{Discussion}

The practical content of this paper is a change in what to measure. Reporting
that a probe recovers a world model at some aggregate accuracy is weak evidence
that the model is load-bearing, because aggregate accuracy is nearly
uninformative about the next action. Measuring the same representation on the
components an action depends on is informative. Any team instrumenting a system
for long-horizon reliability should condition on the state the action uses.

The belief-consistency result reframes the failure. A model that plays a coherent
move in a misremembered position is not behaving erratically, and treatments that
assume erratic behaviour are aimed at the wrong target. Sampling several chains
and voting will not help if every chain is drawn from the same drifted state. A
verifier that scores outputs is well placed to catch the error and badly placed
to prevent it, and it will pay that cost on every query for as long as the system
runs.

The protocol generalises past chess. Anywhere an external checker can reconstruct
the intended intermediate state, program execution traces, spreadsheet
recalculation, structured database updates, ledger reconciliation, the same
measurements are available. The requirement is not a game. It is an oracle.

We also treat the negative results as part of the contribution. A predicted layer
profile came out backwards. A pre-registered metric turned out to be degenerate.
A first design of a second domain turned out to be unlearnable, for a reason that
was diagnosable from the loss curve. Each changed a decision, and each is
reported where it did so.

\section{Implications for Individuals, Organisations, and Society}

\paragraph{For the people who rely on long answers.}
Someone working through a discharge summary, a tenancy agreement, or a benefits
calculation with a model's help is rarely able to audit step twenty-eight. Our
central finding says something uncomfortable about that situation: when state has
drifted, the output is not merely wrong, it is coherent, and coherence is exactly
the signal a non-expert reader uses to decide whether to trust it. The failure is
therefore least visible to the reader least able to check it. A measured horizon,
stated plainly, gives that reader something they do not currently have.

\paragraph{For managers and organisations.}
Long-horizon reliability is a property to be governed rather than a number to be
procured. The decomposition tells a team which remedy fits: a recurring
inference-time cost for a policy problem, or a one-time structural change for a
memory problem. It also supports ordinary practice, triage of workloads that
exceed a measured horizon, human review placed at the depth where reliability
falls rather than spread evenly, and audit evidence that a limit was known and
respected.

\paragraph{Strategic implications.}
An organisation that can measure its own horizon is better placed than one
accepting a vendor's average. The diagnostic needs a rules engine and a linear
probe, not privileged access at scale, so it can be owned and audited in-house.
Treating a measured limit as documentation rather than as an embarrassment turns
a weakness into a defensible position with auditors and regulators.

\paragraph{For society and digital equity.}
Buying reliability with inference-time compute concentrates capability among
those who can afford to spend it on every query. Diagnosing the failure instead,
and fixing what is structural, is the kind of remedy that travels to on-device
assistants and public-sector systems that cannot buy deliberation per request.
The equity question is not whether frontier systems can reason for fifty steps.
It is whether affordable ones can be honest about where they stop.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{description}[nosep]
\item[L1] \textbf{Scale.} Our models are small and trained on a modest corpus,
far below the chess-specific literature and any frontier system. Undertrained
models are easier to study because they fail within a measurable range, which is
what makes the instrument work, but the horizon values describe these models.
Section~\ref{sec:scale} estimates the trend rather than assuming it away.
\item[L2] \textbf{One domain family.} Chess carries an exact oracle by
construction, which is what makes it measurable and also what makes it
unrepresentative of open-ended reasoning. Our attempt at a second domain did not
reach a measurable regime (Section~\ref{sec:synthfail}), so the findings rest on
one instrument and should be read that way.
\item[L3] \textbf{Probe decoding is lossy.} The believed board is an
approximation, which is why we report the legal-move recovery rate as an explicit
ceiling rather than treating the decoded state as ground truth.
\item[L4] \textbf{Pseudo-legality.} Belief consistency is tested with
pseudo-legal moves, since a decoded board may lack a king and make check tests
ill-defined. This is a permissive criterion, which the random-move control is
designed to bound.
\item[L5] \textbf{Scope of the causal claim.} The intervention establishes that
the decoded state direction is read by the policy at the layer tested. It does
not establish which downstream computation consumes it, and a single-direction
edit is a coarse instrument.
\item[L6] \textbf{A metric was revised after seeing data.} Declared in
Section~\ref{sec:results}, applied uniformly, reported alongside the original.
\item[L7] \textbf{No natural-language transfer test.} Establishing that these
findings hold for mathematical or textual reasoning needs matched general-corpus
pre-training, which our compute did not cover. It is absent rather than
approximated.
\end{description}

\section{Avenues of Future Research}

\begin{description}[nosep]
\item[FR1] Extend the scale ladder far enough to estimate how horizon grows with
parameters and data, and test whether the belief-consistency rate is
scale-invariant even where the horizon moves.
\item[FR2] Replicate the decomposition in domains that were not designed as
benchmarks, such as program execution or ledger reconciliation, where an oracle
exists but the data is not constructed.
\item[FR3] Run the natural-language transfer test with matched pre-training, the
experiment that decides whether this line of work reaches practice.
\item[FR4] Move from observation to intervention, editing action-relevant state
directions and measuring whether the induced action follows the induced belief.
\item[FR5] Develop state supervision that does not require an oracle, for example
from execution traces or agreement between sampled continuations.
\item[FR6] Use action-relevant fidelity as a live confidence signal, abstaining or
escalating when the state an action depends on is poorly determined.
\item[FR7] Pre-register horizon metrics on pilot data, so metric selection is
settled before confirmatory runs rather than declared afterwards.
\end{description}

\section{Conclusion}

Long-horizon failure in Transformers is normally measured as one number and
treated with more inference-time compute. Measured against an exact oracle it
separates into two, and in the setting we can measure cleanly, the memory
component dominates and behaves in a specific way. Aggregate world-model accuracy
says little about what a model will do next. The same measurement, restricted to
the state an action depends on, says a great deal. And the failures themselves
are coherent: the model plays a reasonable move in a board it has misremembered.
The people who stand to gain from measuring this are not primarily the builders
of frontier systems. They are the readers who cannot check step twenty-eight, and
the organisations that must decide, with evidence, how far to let a model reason
before a person looks.
